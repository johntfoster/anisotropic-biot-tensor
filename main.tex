\title{An anisotropic Biot tensor from finite-deformation mixture kinematics}
\author{John T. Foster}
\date{}
\hypersetup{pdftitle={An anisotropic Biot tensor from finite-deformation mixture kinematics},pdfauthor={John T. Foster}}

\begin{abstract}
We extend the finite-deformation, single-solid/single-fluid construction of
Foster and Xu to anisotropic skeleton and mineral stiffnesses.  The extension
retains the distention--true-deformation split needed to identify the true
mineral volume ratio and the solid volume fraction.  A pressure Legendre
transform then gives a second-order finite-deformation Biot tensor, defined by
the fixed-pressure sensitivity of true mineral volume to skeleton deformation.
To represent an anisotropic mineral stiffness, the scalar volume ratio is
augmented by a tensorial true-mineral strain state.  A generalized-Hooke
specialization gives the Biot tensor in closed form in terms of a skeleton--
mineral coupling tensor and the mineral stiffness tensor.  The construction
recovers tensorial coefficients for either an anisotropic skeleton or an
anisotropic mineral.  The classical coefficient \(1-K/K_s\) follows in the
fully isotropic case only after a volumetric coupling calibration.  The
coupling tensor is independent constitutive data; a drained skeleton stiffness
and a mineral stiffness alone do not determine it.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} finite-deformation poromechanics; anisotropic
elasticity; Biot tensor; mineral stiffness; Legendre transform

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The linear theory of a fluid-saturated anisotropic porous solid was formulated
by Biot \citep{biot1955anisotropic}.  Its pressure--stress coupling requires,
in addition to the fourth-order elastic tensor, a symmetric second-order
effective-stress (Biot--Willis) tensor and a storage coefficient
\citep{biotwillis1957,thompsonwillis1991}.  This distinction is consequential:
anisotropic elastic stiffness alone does not determine the directional
pressure coupling.  Micromechanical interpretations and practical
identification procedures have made this point explicit under assumptions
about the solid constituents and pore structure \citep{cheng1997}, while
crack-based analyses show how pore fabric can generate anisotropic Biot--Willis
and Skempton tensors \citep{wong2017}.

The experimental literature also motivates retaining tensorial coupling for
layered rocks.  Dynamic measurements on VTI shale establish direction- and
stress-dependent elastic stiffnesses \citep{sviridov2017}, and a more complete
set of drained, pore-pressure, and deviatoric tests on Callovo--Oxfordian
claystone found a strongly anisotropic strain response and a markedly
anisotropic Skempton response, even though the back-calculated Biot-tensor
anisotropy was small in that material \citep{braun2020}.  At the fracture
scale, hydraulic-fracture models for shale must couple crack pressurization
with pore-pressure diffusion and the stress carried by the solid phase
\citep{chaubazantsu2016}.  That work is an application motivation for
anisotropic poromechanical models with tensorial pressure coupling.

The Biot coefficient measures the pressure contribution to the total stress
of a saturated porous solid.  Foster and Xu \citep{fosterxu2025} derive a
tensorial fixed-pressure sensitivity of the intrinsic solid specific volume,
\(\bar v_s\), to skeleton deformation from mixture kinematics,
intrinsic-density conjugacy, and a pressure Legendre transform.  Their scalar
specialization closes the material mass balance by taking
\(\bar v_s=\bar v_s(J)\), so the solid volume fraction, \(\phi_s\), is also a
function of \(J\) alone and the sensitivity reduces to a scalar coefficient.

Allowing the solid volume fraction to depend on the skeleton deformation
gradient, \(\phi_s=\phi_s(\F,\ldots)\), makes \(\bar v_s\) depend on
\(\F\) beyond \(J\), and its fixed-pressure sensitivity remains tensorial.

Here, we generalize this construction for one deformable solid and one fluid
in a saturated material at local pressure equilibrium.  We retain the
finite-deformation kinematics and Legendre-transform argument, so the result
does not depend on a particular elastic law.  We then give a small-strain realization using a
generalized-Hooke energy.  This realization separates the skeleton stiffness,
the mineral stiffness, and the stiffness that couples the two states.  The
coupling stiffness is needed because the two individual stiffness tensors do
not say how skeleton strain changes the mineral state at fixed pore pressure.
Finally, we show how the familiar isotropic coefficient follows from a
calibrated volumetric specialization consistent with the Foster--Xu
construction.

\section{Kinematics and thermodynamic setting}
\label{sec:kinematics}

Let \(\F\) map the solid skeleton reference configuration to its current
configuration, and let \(J=\det\F>0\) be the corresponding skeleton volume
ratio.  The solid partial density is
\(\rho_s=\phi_s\bar\rho_s\), where \(\phi_s\) is the solid volume fraction
and \(\bar\rho_s\) is the intrinsic density of the mineral solid.  For a
uniform reference state, solid mass conservation gives
\begin{equation}
 J\rho_s=\rho_{s0}=\phi_{s0}\bar\rho_{s0}.
 \label{eq:solid-mass}
\end{equation}
The true mineral volume ratio is
\begin{equation}
 \bar J\equiv\frac{\bar\rho_{s0}}{\bar\rho_s},
 \qquad
 \phi_s=\frac{\phi_{s0}\bar J}{J},
 \qquad \phi_f=1-\phi_s.
 \label{eq:distention}
\end{equation}
Thus \(\bar J\) is the mineral volume ratio, \(\phi_f\) is the fluid volume
fraction, and \(J/\bar J\) is the distention ratio of the skeleton.  In other
words, \(J\) measures the change of the porous skeleton volume, whereas
\(\bar J\) measures the change of volume of the mineral itself.  We separate
these two changes using the
distention--true-deformation construction emphasized by Drumheller
\citep{drumheller2000},
\begin{equation}
 \F=\A\Fb,
 \qquad \det\A=\frac{J}{\bar J},
 \qquad \det\Fb=\bar J.
\label{eq:true-mineral-jacobian}
\end{equation}
Here \(\A\) describes distention and \(\Fb\) describes true mineral
deformation.  The determinant relation in
\eqref{eq:true-mineral-jacobian} is enough to track volume, but it does not
determine the shape part of the mineral deformation.  To model anisotropic
minerals, we therefore also need an objective energy and a local problem that
determines the mineral state.
For the nonreacting, one-solid/one-fluid specialization considered here, an
isotropic distention \(\A=a\I\), with
\(a=(J/\bar J)^{1/3}\), makes \(\F=a\Fb\) kinematically equivalent, up to
the naming and ordering of factors, to the isotropic swelling--elastic
multiplicative split used in Flory-type network theories \citep{flory1961}.
This is a kinematic equivalence only: it neither transfers Flory's mixing free
energy nor supplies the present pressure-conjugacy or constitutive assumptions.

Let the local mineral coordinates be \(\bm z=(q,\bm\xi)\), where
\(q=\ln\bar J\) and \(\bm\xi\) are isochoric mineral-shape coordinates.
At the fixed temperature considered here, let
\(\psi^s(\F,q,\bm\xi)\) be the solid specific Helmholtz free energy and
define the mixture-reference free-energy density by
\(W(\F,q,\bm\xi)=\rho_{s0}\psi^s(\F,q,\bm\xi)\).  The normalization is
that of Foster and Xu \citep{fosterxu2025}: their intrinsic solid specific
volume is \(\bar v_s=1/\bar\rho_s=\bar v_{s0}\bar J\), and
\(\rho_{s0}\bar v_{s0}=\phi_{s0}\).  The isothermal pressure conjugacy
\(\partial\psi^s/\partial\bar v_s=-p\) then gives the following
mixture-reference relations.  Here, \(q\) records the mineral volume change
and \(\bm\xi\) records its shape change without an additional change of
volume.  Structural tensors and history variables are suppressed and held
fixed below.  Local pressure equilibrium determines the volume coordinate,
while shape equilibrium determines the isochoric coordinates:
\begin{equation}
 \left.\pder{W}{q}\right|_{\F,\bm\xi}=-\phi_{s0}p\bar J,
 \qquad
 \left.\pder{W}{\bm\xi}\right|_{\F,q}=\bm0.
 \label{eq:mineral-coordinate-equilibrium}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the volume derivative at fixed \(\F\) and \(\bm\xi\) is
\begin{equation}
 \left.\pder{W}{\bar J}\right|_{\F,\bm\xi}=-\phi_{s0}p.
 \label{eq:pressure-conjugacy}
\end{equation}
Consider an already-deformed specimen that is rotated rigidly in space, with
its shape and pore pressure left unchanged.  This change of laboratory
orientation cannot affect the stored energy or the mineral state selected by
local equilibrium.  Thus, the mineral volume ratio \(\bar J\) and the material
shape coordinates \(\bm\xi\) do not depend on how the specimen is oriented.

\section{Finite-deformation Biot tensor}
\label{sec:finite-biot}

Assume that \eqref{eq:mineral-coordinate-equilibrium} can be solved locally
for \((q,\bm\xi)=(q,\bm\xi)(\F,p)\).  Specifically, the Jacobian of
\(\bigl(W_q+\phi_{s0}p\bar J,W_{\bm\xi}\bigr)\) with respect to
\((q,\bm\xi)\) is assumed nonsingular.  Define the reduced fixed-pressure energy and its
Legendre transform by
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:fixed-pressure-energies}
\begin{align}
 W^{\prime\prime}(\F,p)&=W\bigl(\F,q(\F,p),\bm\xi(\F,p)\bigr),
 \label{eq:reduced-energy}\\
 W^{\prime}(\F,p)&=W^{\prime\prime}(\F,p)+\phi_{s0}p\,
 \bar J\bigl(q(\F,p)\bigr).
 \label{eq:legendre-energy}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
Foster and Xu \citep[eq.~(33)]{fosterxu2025} transform their specific internal
energy according to
\(\widetilde e^s(\F,p)=e^s(\F,\bar v_s)+p\bar v_s\).  Equation
\eqref{eq:legendre-energy} is its isothermal Helmholtz counterpart.  Replacing
\(e^s\) by \(\psi^s\), multiplying by \(\rho_{s0}\), and using
\(\bar v_s=\bar v_{s0}\bar J\) together with
\(\rho_{s0}\bar v_{s0}=\phi_{s0}\) gives
\(W+\phi_{s0}p\bar J=\rho_{s0}(\psi^s+p\bar v_s)\).  Evaluating this
identity at the local solution \((q,\bm\xi)(\F,p)\) gives
\eqref{eq:legendre-energy} per unit reference mixture volume.
The first Piola stresses conjugate to \(\F\), respectively at a fixed mineral
state and at a fixed pore pressure, are
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:effective-stresses}
\begin{align}
 \bm P^{\prime}&=\left.\pder{W}{\F}\right|_{q,\bm\xi}
 =\left.\pder{W^{\prime}}{\F}\right|_p,
 \label{eq:fixed-mineral-state-stress}\\
 \bm P^{\prime\prime}&=\left.\pder{W^{\prime\prime}}{\F}\right|_p.
 \label{eq:fixed-pressure-stress}
\end{align}
\end{subequations}
The second equality in the first definition follows because the changes in
\(q\) and \(\bm\xi\) cancel through
\eqref{eq:mineral-coordinate-equilibrium}.  Differentiating
\eqref{eq:legendre-energy} gives
\begin{equation}
 \bm P^{\prime}=
 \bm P^{\prime\prime}+
 \phi_{s0}p\left.\pder{\bar J}{\F}\right|_p.
 \label{eq:piola-transform-general}
\end{equation}

The total mixture first Piola stress is
\(\bm P=\bm P^{\prime}-pJ\F^{-T}\).  The first term contains the stress
transmitted through the skeleton and mineral state, while the second is the
usual pore-pressure traction.  We define the finite-deformation Biot tensor in
the current configuration by
\begin{equation}
 \bm B
 =\I-\frac{\phi_{s0}}{J}
 \left.\pder{\bar J}{\F}\right|_p\F^T.
 \label{eq:finite-biot-tensor}
\end{equation}
Equations \eqref{eq:piola-transform-general} and \eqref{eq:finite-biot-tensor}
then give the familiar effective-stress form
\begin{equation}
 \bm\sigma=\bm\sigma^{\prime\prime}-p\bm B,
 \label{eq:total-stress-transform}
\end{equation}
where \(\bm\sigma^{\prime\prime}=J^{-1}\bm P^{\prime\prime}\F^T\).
Under the rotation-invariance assumption stated above, a superposed rigid
rotation \(\bm Q\) gives \(\bm B(\bm Q\F,p)=\bm Q\bm B(\F,p)\bm Q^T\),
and the same relation holds for \(\bm\sigma^{\prime\prime}\).  For comparison,
suppose that the mineral volume responds only to skeleton volume and not to
skeleton shape.  Then \(\bar J\) depends on \(\F\) only through \(J\), and
\begin{equation}
 \bm B=\left(1-\phi_{s0}\left.\pder{\bar J}{J}\right|_p\right)\I,
 \label{eq:scalar-recovery}
\end{equation}
which is the scalar finite-deformation result of Foster and Xu
\citep[eq.~(39)]{fosterxu2025}.
Their normalization gives the same expression exactly: their reference
specific volume is \(v_{s0}=1/\rho_{s0}\), where
\(\rho_{s0}=\phi_{s0}\bar\rho_{s0}\), whereas the intrinsic specific volume
is \(\bar v_s=\bar J/\bar\rho_{s0}\).  Hence
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\bar v_s}{v_{s0}}=\phi_{s0}\bar J,
 \qquad
 B=1-\phi_{s0}\left.\pder{\bar J}{J}\right|_p.
 \label{eq:foster-xu-normalization-bridge}
\end{equation}
This is their equation~(39), expressed using the present volume ratio.

The tensor in \eqref{eq:finite-biot-tensor} is symmetric under the stated
rotation-invariance assumption.  Indeed, take a superposed infinitesimal rotation
\(\bm Q=\I+\bm\Omega\), \(\bm\Omega^T=-\bm\Omega\).  Invariance of
\(\bar J\) gives
\begin{equation}
 \left.\pder{\bar J}{\F}\right|_p:(\bm\Omega\F)=0,
 \qquad\text{so}\qquad
 \left.\pder{\bar J}{\F}\right|_p\F^T
 \ \text{is symmetric}.
 \label{eq:biot-tensor-symmetry}
\end{equation}

\section{Generalized-Hooke specialization}
\label{sec:hooke}

The small-strain limit makes the constitutive information required for the
tensorial coefficient explicit.  Let
\(\bm e=\sym\nabla\bm u\) be the skeleton strain and let \(\bm\eta\) be a
symmetric, material tangent coordinate for the true-mineral deformation.  For
example, with \(\Fb=\bm R_b\bm U_b\), take
\(\bm\eta=\log\bm U_b\); then \(\tr\bm\eta=q=\ln\bar J\) exactly, and
\(\bar J=1+\tr\bm\eta+o(\lVert\bm\eta\rVert)\).  Consider the quadratic energy
\begin{equation}
 W(\bm e,\bm\eta)=
 \frac12\bm e:\C:\bm e+
 \bm e:\D:\bm\eta+
 \frac12\bm\eta:\Hh:\bm\eta,
 \label{eq:quadratic-energy}
\end{equation}
All three tensors have units of pressure.  \(\C\) and \(\Hh\) are self-adjoint
maps on symmetric tensors (minor and major symmetries); \(\D^T\) denotes the
adjoint of \(\D\) under the double contraction.  The tensors respectively
describe the constrained skeleton stiffness, skeleton--mineral coupling, and
mineral stiffness.  A stable quadratic energy requires \(\Hh\) positive definite
and its Schur complement
\(\C^d=\C-\D:\Hh^{-1}:\D^T\) positive definite on symmetric skeleton strains.

Mineral equilibrium under pore pressure is
\begin{equation}
 \D^T:\bm e+\Hh:\bm\eta=-\phi_{s0}p\I.
 \label{eq:linear-mineral-equilibrium}
\end{equation}
It follows that
\begin{equation}
 \bm\eta=-\Hh^{-1}:\D^T:\bm e
 -\phi_{s0}p\,\Hh^{-1}:\I.
 \label{eq:mineral-strain-solution}
\end{equation}
At \(p=0\), elimination of \(\bm\eta\) gives the drained skeleton stiffness
\begin{equation}
 \C^d=\C-\D:\Hh^{-1}:\D^T.
 \label{eq:drained-stiffness}
\end{equation}
Thus a desired \(\C^d\) can be imposed by setting
\(\C=\C^d+\D:\Hh^{-1}:\D^T\).  This observation also shows why \(\C^d\)
and \(\Hh\) alone are insufficient: \(\D\) remains independent data.

Since \(\bar J=1+\tr\bm\eta\) to first order, \eqref{eq:mineral-strain-solution}
and \eqref{eq:finite-biot-tensor} yield the small-strain Biot tensor
\begin{equation}
 \boxed{\quad
 \bm B_0=\I+
 \phi_{s0}\,\I:\Hh^{-1}:\D^T.
 \quad}
 \label{eq:linear-biot-tensor}
\end{equation}
The stress relation is \(\bm\sigma=\bm\sigma^{\prime\prime}-p\bm B_0\).
For symmetric strain spaces and the stated adjoint maps,
\(\bm B_0\) is symmetric; this is also the small-strain limit of
\eqref{eq:biot-tensor-symmetry}.

\subsection{Matched logarithmic branch}
\label{sec:matched-branch}

The stable quadratic family above is broader than the Foster--Xu-compatible
matched volumetric specialization adopted in this paper.  The Foster--Xu result supports the
pressure conjugacy, Legendre transformation, and scalar finite-deformation
coefficient used above \citep{fosterxu2025}; the tensorial extension and the
following matched branch are constitutive choices of the present paper.  Write
the volumetric energy as
\begin{equation}
 W_{\mathrm{vol}}=
 \frac12 C_{\mathrm{vol}}(\tr\bm e)^2+
 D_{\mathrm{vol}}(\tr\bm e)(\tr\bm\eta)+
 \frac12 H_{\mathrm{vol}}(\tr\bm\eta)^2.
 \label{eq:matched-volumetric-energy}
\end{equation}
For the matched logarithmic skeleton and mineral laws adopted here, its
coefficients are
\begin{equation}
 C_{\mathrm{vol}}=\frac{K}{1-r},\qquad
 D_{\mathrm{vol}}=-\frac{K}{1-r},\qquad
 H_{\mathrm{vol}}=\frac{\phi_{s0}K_s}{1-r},\qquad
 r=\frac{K}{\phi_{s0}K_s}.
 \label{eq:matched-volumetric-coefficients}
\end{equation}
Here \(K\) and \(K_s\) are the drained skeleton and mineral bulk moduli.
For \(0<r<1\), the mineral volumetric stiffness and the drained Schur
complement are positive.  Indeed,
\begin{equation}
 C_{\mathrm{vol}}-\frac{D_{\mathrm{vol}}^2}{H_{\mathrm{vol}}}=K.
 \label{eq:matched-drained-bulk}
\end{equation}
The branch therefore has the prescribed drained bulk modulus and is a
particular calibrated member of \eqref{eq:quadratic-energy}, rather than the
only stable quadratic family.  It specifies only volumetric blocks.  The
deviatoric blocks must be chosen separately with positive mineral stiffness
and a positive drained Schur complement; setting \(\D_{\mathrm{dev}}=\bm0\)
is one admissible choice.

\section{Special cases}
\label{sec:special-cases}

\subsection{Isotropic mineral and anisotropic skeleton}

Let the mineral stiffness be isotropic,
\begin{equation}
 \Hh=\phi_{s0}\left[
 \lambda_s\,\I\otimes\I+2\mu_s\,\mathbb I_{\mathrm{sym}}
 \right],
 \qquad K_s=\lambda_s+\frac23\mu_s,
 \label{eq:isotropic-mineral-stiffness}
\end{equation}
while \(\C^d\) and \(\D\) may be anisotropic.  Since
\(\I:\Hh^{-1}=\I/(3\phi_{s0}K_s)\), \eqref{eq:linear-biot-tensor} becomes
\begin{equation}
 \bm B_0=\I+\frac{1}{3K_s}\,\I:\D^T.
 \label{eq:isotropic-mineral-biot-tensor}
\end{equation}
The skeleton can therefore be anisotropic even when the mineral is isotropic.
Its directional pressure coupling is carried by the volumetric mineral--
skeleton coupling \(\I:\D^T\), not by \(\C^d\) alone.

\subsection{Isotropic drained skeleton stiffness with anisotropic mineral or coupling}

Conversely, let \(\C^d\) be isotropic with Lam\'{e} moduli
\(\lambda\) and \(\mu\), while \(\Hh\) is anisotropic.  Equation
\eqref{eq:linear-biot-tensor} remains
\begin{equation}
 \bm B_0=\I+\phi_{s0}\I:\Hh^{-1}:\D^T.
 \label{eq:anisotropic-mineral-biot-tensor}
\end{equation}
An isotropic drained skeleton therefore does not impose an isotropic Biot
tensor.  An anisotropic mineral compliance or coupling can produce directional
pressure coupling through the right-hand side of
\eqref{eq:anisotropic-mineral-biot-tensor}.

Introduce the isotropic projectors
\(\Pvol=\tfrac13\I\otimes\I\) and
\(\Pdev=\mathbb I_{\mathrm{sym}}-\Pvol\).  For a mineral-only anisotropy
subcase, take an isotropic coupling
\(\D=d_v\Pvol+d_d\Pdev\) and an anisotropic positive-definite \(\Hh\).
Then \(\I:\Hh^{-1}:\D^T\) need not be spherical, so mineral compliance alone
can make \(\bm B_0\) anisotropic even though both \(\C^d\) and \(\D\) are
isotropic.  The drained Schur complement condition remains required.

\subsection{Fully isotropic, calibrated limit}

Let the drained skeleton stiffness be
\begin{equation}
 \C^d=\lambda\,\I\otimes\I+2\mu\,\mathbb I_{\mathrm{sym}},
 \qquad K=\lambda+\frac23\mu,
 \label{eq:isotropic-drained-stiffness}
\end{equation}
and use the isotropic mineral stiffness \eqref{eq:isotropic-mineral-stiffness}.
This is an illustrative uncoupled isotropic mineral Hessian, with
\(H_{\mathrm{vol}}=\phi_{s0}K_s\).  The general isotropic coupling has
independent volumetric and deviatoric parts,
\begin{equation}
 \D=d_v\Pvol+d_d\Pdev,
 \label{eq:general-isotropic-coupling}
\end{equation}
Writing \(\Hh=h_v\Pvol+h_d\Pdev\), with
\(h_v=3\phi_{s0}K_s\), gives
\begin{equation}
 \bm B_0=\left(1+\phi_{s0}\frac{d_v}{h_v}\right)\I.
 \label{eq:general-isotropic-biot}
\end{equation}
Thus isotropy alone leaves the scalar coupling undetermined.  The precise
ratio calibration required for the classical coefficient is
\begin{equation}
 \frac{d_v}{h_v}=-\frac{K}{\phi_{s0}K_s}.
 \label{eq:classical-coupling-calibration}
\end{equation}
For the illustrative Hessian, it reads \(d_v=-3K\), equivalently
\(D_{\mathrm{vol}}=-K\).
Under \eqref{eq:classical-coupling-calibration},
\begin{equation}
 \bm B_0=\left(1-\frac{K}{K_s}\right)\I,
 \qquad B=1-\frac{K}{K_s}.
 \label{eq:classical-biot}
\end{equation}
The matched branch \eqref{eq:matched-volumetric-coefficients} is distinct:
it has \(H_{\mathrm{vol}}=\phi_{s0}K_s/(1-r)\) and
\(D_{\mathrm{vol}}=-K/(1-r)\).  Its tensor representation
\(d_v=3D_{\mathrm{vol}}\), \(h_v=3H_{\mathrm{vol}}\) has the same ratio in
\eqref{eq:classical-coupling-calibration}, and consequently the same
reduction.  The deviatoric coupling \(d_d\) does not enter \(\bm B_0\), but
it remains relevant to stability and effective stress.
The conventional restriction \(0\leq B\leq1\) follows under the usual
isotropic stability and ordering assumptions, including \(0\leq K\leq K_s\).
Those inequalities are material restrictions and are not consequences of
tensor anisotropy alone.

\section{Discussion and conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

Foster and Xu establish the scalar pressure Legendre transform.  The present
extension produces a tensorial Biot correction whenever true mineral volume
has a directional, fixed-pressure response to skeleton deformation.  The
distention relation
\eqref{eq:distention} remains the kinematic link between intrinsic density,
solid volume fraction, and skeleton volume.  A tensorial true-mineral state is
needed only to model an anisotropic mineral stiffness; it is not an assertion
of a particular microscopic deformation map.

The generalized-Hooke model identifies the minimal additional constitutive
ingredient: \(\D\) calibrates the skeleton--mineral coupling.  It permits
anisotropic skeleton and mineral stiffness walls and recovers
\eqref{eq:classical-biot} under the explicit calibration
\eqref{eq:classical-coupling-calibration}.  A finite-deformation
implementation requires an objective hyperelastic energy, a locally solvable
mineral stationarity problem, and verification of the resulting tangent and
stress transform.  These requirements delimit the present theoretical result
and provide the constitutive starting point for an anisotropic numerical model.
