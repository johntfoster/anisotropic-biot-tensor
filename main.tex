\begin{abstract}
We derive the finite-deformation Biot tensor of a saturated elastic porous
solid from the volume-fraction-weighted phase stresses and their reversible
work. We write the distention gradient as a dilation times a proper rotation, so
mineral deformation and changes in pore volume are distinguished without
fixing the orientation of the intermediate frame. For an objective mineral
energy and a distention energy depending only on volume, the internal
rotation cancels from the constitutive response. The mineral stress must
nevertheless be rotated into the mixture frame before the phase stresses
are added. A generalized Hooke law in material logarithmic strain gives an
implicit mineral-volume equation and an explicit pressure-coupling tensor.
The drained compliance equals the mineral compliance divided by the
reference solid fraction, plus a spherical, rank-one contribution.
Mineral volume--shape coupling produces directional
pressure sensitivity, whereas an isotropic mineral recovers the scalar
nonlinear Biot coefficient. Numerical experiments compare these responses
under pressure, shear, and constrained extension. Independent energy and
pressure derivatives verify the complete spatial phase-stress balance and
its invariance under changes of the internal rotation. A finite-element
implementation of the coupled deformation and fluid-transport balances is
verified against a manufactured solution for the constant reference tangent
and, in the same limit, the constant-coefficient consolidation reference, and
exercised on rotated-anisotropy
and partial-drainage demonstrations at finite load; no quantitative
finite-deformation verification and no experimental validation are claimed for
those demonstrations. The tensorial distention law is implemented in its
reference-state linearization and checked at the material point against a
separate re-implementation
of the section equations that shares the same modelling conventions, and
demonstrated in a coupled calculation whose
pressure response rotates with the pore fabric behind an isotropic mineral.
The conformal construction
isolates the anisotropy inherited from the mineral. Replacing the scalar
dilation with a symmetric positive definite distention whose unimodular
part is a pore-fabric tensor supplies the additional constitutive law for
shape-changing distention. With the pore-fabric orientation prescribed as
material data and no relative rotation of the fabric and the mineral matrix
represented, it relaxes the rank-one drained-compliance restriction to a
fabric-symmetric compliance supported on the retained volumetric--axial
distention subspace and yields an anisotropic Biot tensor driven by both
mineral and pore-fabric anisotropy.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} anisotropic poroelasticity; finite deformation;
Biot tensor; mineral compression; logarithmic strain; finite-element
verification

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A change in pore pressure deforms both the pore space and the solid material
surrounding it. In an anisotropic porous solid, the stress needed to hold
the specimen fixed can change by different amounts in different directions.
Biot's anisotropic theory expresses this response through a tensor coupling
pore pressure to stress \citep{biot1955anisotropic}. The reference elastic
coefficients describe this coupling together with deformation and fluid
storage \citep{biotwillis1957,thompsonwillis1991}. At finite deformation,
mineral compression changes the solid volume fraction and therefore the
weights with which the phase stresses contribute to the mixture stress.

The relation between pressure and volume supplies a direct way to follow
these changes. De Buhan et al.\ \citep{debuhan1998} formulate finite-strain
poromechanics using deformation and pore volume. Foster and Xu
\citep{fosterxu2025} express the Biot coefficient through the change in
intrinsic solid volume with skeleton volume at fixed pore pressure.
Gajo \citep{gajo2010} combines logarithmic volumetric laws for the skeleton
and compressible solid constituent in a finite-strain hyperelastic model.
The elastic scalar equation used in our companion manuscript
\citep{foster2026poroplastic} follows from the same logarithmic relations.
We follow the same physical sequence: phase stress balance, reversible
work, equivalent energy, and implicit differentiation of mineral volume.
Anisotropic poroelastic coefficients are measured in shales, claystones, and
cracked rock and are related to laboratory stiffnesses
\citep{cheng1997,wong2017,braun2021,sviridov2017,makhnenkolabuz2016}, and they
enter continuum frameworks for coupled deformation and flow in anisotropic
elastoplastic porous media \citep{zhaoborja2020}. Large deformations of soft
porous materials and hydraulic crack growth raise related questions about solid
and pore-volume change \citep{macminnetal2016,chaubazantsu2016}, for which
polymer-network thermodynamics supplies classical volumetric relations
\citep{flory1961}.

A second-rank fabric tensor condenses the orientation and shape of the
pore space. Cowin showed that the elasticity tensor of a solid with a
known fabric is an isotropic function of that fabric, so a second-rank
fabric tensor determines the anisotropic elastic constants
\citep{cowin1985fabric,turnercowin1987}, and a symmetry-invariant form of
that relation is given by Moesen, Cardoso, and Cowin
\citep{moesencardosocowin2012}. The same construction extends to
poroelasticity, where the fabric supplies the anisotropic poroelastic
coefficients \citep{cowin2004fabric,cowinmehrabadi2007}, again by assigning
the fabric-dependent coefficients to the solid directly, and at small strain;
Thompson and
Willis reformulate the anisotropic poroelasticity equations in a form that
exposes the tensor structure of that coupling
\citep{thompsonwillis1991}, and oriented cracks provide a concrete fabric
whose elastic consequences are described by Hudson \citep{hudson1981}.

Reactions do more than change pore volume: they create and orient the pore
structure. Serpentinization and carbonation of peridotite generate
reaction-induced cracking whose porosity and geometry are measured by
three-dimensional microtomography \citep{rudgekelemen2010,jons2017}, and
volatile-consuming reactions fracture rock and self-accelerate fluid flow
\citep{uno2022}. At the mineral scale, interface-coupled dissolution and
precipitation replace one solid by another while preserving and shaping
the pore space, a pseudomorphic process documented across silicate,
carbonate, and oxide systems
\citep{putnis2002,putnis2009,ruizagudo2014,geisler2007,altreewilliams2015}.

Together these observations establish two facts: fabric, not porosity
alone, controls the effective poroelastic properties, and reactions create
and orient pore structure. Among the fabric-elasticity and
fabric-poroelasticity constructions surveyed here, we are not aware of a
tensorial distention law, a constitutive relation stating how the shape and
orientation of the pore space enter a finite-deformation poromechanical
energy and the Biot tensor derived from it. The route taken here is
distinct from the fabric-elasticity and fabric-poroelasticity constructions
above: the fabric tensor does not stand in for anisotropic elastic
constants of the solid, but enters the distention energy of the pore space,
from which the anisotropic Biot tensor follows through the mineral-volume
response. \Cref{sec:pore-fabric}
derives that law by extending the volume-only conformal distention to a
symmetric positive definite distention whose unimodular part is the pore
fabric.

We consider an anisotropic mineral and allow the distention gradient
\(\mathbf A\) of \eqref{eq:true-mineral-jacobian}, with \(J=a\bar J\), to
contain both a dilation and a proper rotation. The dilation changes pore
volume while leaving the shape of the mineral unchanged. The rotation
changes only the frame in which the mineral stress is represented.
Carrying that rotation through the virtual work identifies the phase
stress in the mixture frame and determines whether it affects the
constitutive response. Applying the complete mineral Hooke law then gives both the
volumetric work and the shear work. These requirements restrict the two
elastic stiffness tensors: the additional drained compliance must produce
only a spherical change in strain. We derive that restriction rather than prescribe
an independent tensor describing pressure coupling.

The result is an elastic constitutive model for a specified deformation
mechanism. Pore-scale calculations offer a separate way to determine
effective coefficients from geometry \citep{dehghanipentamerodio2019,
dehghanizilian2021}. In the present model, the mineral law and volume-only conformal
distention are prescribed directly. After deriving the compatible energy,
we obtain the mineral-volume equation and the Biot tensor, examine their
reference and isotropic limits, and verify the full phase stress balance
numerically. We then implement the coupled deformation and fluid-transport
balances, verify the implementation against a manufactured solution in the
constant reference tangent and, in the same limit, the constant-coefficient
consolidation reference, and report finite-load demonstrations. We restrict attention to elastic
deformation.

\section{Deformation and volume-fraction-weighted stress}
\label{sec:kinematics}

Let \(\mathbf F\) be the skeleton deformation gradient and
\(J=\det\mathbf F>0\) its volume ratio. We separate true mineral
deformation from distention using \citep{drumheller2000}
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf F=\mathbf A\bar{\mathbf F},\qquad
 J=a\bar J,\qquad
 a=\det\mathbf A,\qquad \bar J=\det\bar{\mathbf F}.
 \label{eq:true-mineral-jacobian}
\end{equation}
Here \(\bar J\) measures mineral volume change and \(a\) measures the
additional volume change associated with the pore structure. The reference
state has \(J=\bar J=a=1\). We specialize the distention gradient to
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf A=a^{1/3}\mathbf R_A,\qquad
 \mathbf R_A^T\mathbf R_A=\mathbf I,\qquad \det\mathbf R_A=1,\qquad
 \bar{\mathbf F}=a^{-1/3}\mathbf R_A^T\mathbf F.
 \label{eq:spherical-distention}
\end{equation}
Here \(\mathbf R_A\) is a proper rotation and \(a\) is the determinant
of \(\mathbf A\), not a principal stretch. The inverse distention gradient
is \(a^{-1/3}\mathbf R_A^T\). This specialization allows a relative
rotation and a change in volume, with no shape-changing distention.
In particular, the mineral right Cauchy--Green tensor satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \bar{\mathbf C}=\bar{\mathbf F}^T\bar{\mathbf F}
                  =a^{-2/3}\mathbf C,\qquad
 \mathbf C=\mathbf F^T\mathbf F.
 \label{eq:conformal-mineral-metric}
\end{equation}
Consequently the normalized right stretches of the mineral and skeleton
are equal. A general determinant-one distention factor would additionally allow
shear and describe a different deformation mechanism.

Let \(\bar\rho_s\) be the solid intrinsic density, \(\phi_s\) the current
solid volume fraction, and \(\rho_s=\phi_s\bar\rho_s\) the solid mass
per current mixture volume. Throughout, a bar on a kinematic or energetic quantity denotes the mineral
state reached by removing the distention in the general multiplicative
decomposition \eqref{eq:true-mineral-jacobian}, of which the conformal factor
\eqref{eq:spherical-distention} is the specialization.
A bar on an intrinsic density denotes the per-phase-volume value:
\(\bar\rho_s=\rho_s/\phi_s\) for the solid, which is the mineral state, and
\(\bar\rho_f=\rho_f/\phi_f\) for the fluid.
A bar on a stress denotes its representation in the mixture frame, whereas a hat denotes
the true frame with the mineral normalization. The barred stresses also carry different normalizations:
\(\bar{\mathbf\sigma}_s\) is per current mineral volume,
\(\bar{\mathbf\tau}_s=\bar J\bar{\mathbf\sigma}_s\) of
\eqref{eq:kirchhoff-volume-conventions} is per reference mineral volume.
When the bar is written on the distention stress \(\bar{\mathbf S}_{\mathrm{dis}}\)
of \eqref{eq:fabric-distention-stress} it instead marks the member of the
work-conjugate pair that is conjugate to the distention tensor \(\mathbf G\),
per reference mixture volume in the intermediate frame; its unbarred partner
\(\mathbf S_{\mathrm{dis}}\) is conjugate to the distention logarithmic
strain \(\mathbf E_{\mathrm{dis}}\) in that same frame and normalization. A bar on a
prescribed boundary datum, such as the mass flux
\(\bar Q_f\) of \eqref{eq:fe-fluid-residual}, denotes its prescribed value on that
boundary. A single prime denotes the effective stress that carries the full
pore-pressure term, as in \(\mathbf\sigma'\) of
\eqref{eq:total-cauchy-single-prime}, and a double prime denotes its reduced
counterpart at the prescribed pressure, the stress \(\mathbf P''\) of
\eqref{eq:fixed-pressure-stress}, so that
\(\mathbf\sigma=\mathbf\sigma''-p\mathbf B\) in
\eqref{eq:finite-biot-tensor}. The same two marks name the two prescribed-pressure potentials of
\cref{sec:finite-biot}: the reduced potential
\(W''\) of \eqref{eq:reduced-energy}, whose prescribed-pressure derivative is
the double-primed stress \(\mathbf P''\) of \eqref{eq:fixed-pressure-stress},
and its single-primed companion
\(W'\) of \eqref{eq:legendre-energy}, whose prescribed-pressure derivative
\(\mathbf P'\) of \eqref{eq:fixed-mineral-state-stress} carries the full
pore-pressure term.
A superscript \(d\) denotes the drained skeleton, as in the drained
stiffness \(\mathbb{C}^d\) of \eqref{eq:drained-stiffness-restriction} and
the drained energy \(W^d\) of \eqref{eq:prescribed-logarithmic-energies}. The
distention quantities instead carry the subscript \(\mathrm{dis}\):
\(W_{\mathrm{dis}}\), \(\mathbf E_{\mathrm{dis}}\), and
\(\mathbf S_{\mathrm{dis}}\) in \cref{sec:pore-fabric}, together with the
scalar volume-only distention energy \(W_A(a)\) of \cref{sec:work-equivalence},
of which \(W_{\mathrm{dis}}\) is the tensorial generalization; both energies are
normalized per reference mixture volume. The two labels are
kept distinct throughout, so \(d\) never means distention. A superscript \(n\)
marks the discrete time level of \cref{sec:finite-elements}, and a subscript \(0\)
marks a quantity of the reference configuration, as in \(\phi_{s0}\) and
\(\bar\rho_{s0}\). A wide tilde denotes the representation of a mixture-frame
quantity in the true (mineral) frame, obtained by the inverse rotation
\(\mathbf R_A^T\), as in \(\widetilde{\mathbf\tau}=\mathbf R_A^T\mathbf\tau'\mathbf R_A\) of
\eqref{eq:fabric-phase-work}. The hat and the wide tilde both place their
object in that same true frame, so the criterion that separates them is
normalization, not frame: a hat marks a mineral-normalized quantity, per
mineral volume, as in the intrinsic mineral stresses
\(\widehat{\mathbf\sigma}_s\), per current mineral volume, and
\(\widehat{\mathbf\tau}_s\), per reference mineral volume; a wide tilde marks a
mixture-normalized quantity rotated into the true frame, as in
\(\widetilde{\mathbf\tau}\), whose partner \(\mathbf\tau'\) is the skeleton
effective Kirchhoff stress per reference mixture volume. A mineral-normalized
stress therefore keeps its hat in the true frame however it is reached, so
\(\widehat{\mathbf\tau}_s=\mathbf R_A^T\bar{\mathbf\tau}_s\mathbf R_A\) in
\eqref{eq:rotated-mineral-kirchhoff}, and no tilde is written for it.
The mixture-frame and true-frame stress representations differ by the rotation \eqref{eq:rotated-mineral-cauchy}.
Fourth-order tensors are set in blackboard bold and second-order tensors in
upright bold. Conservation of solid mass gives
\begin{align}
 J\rho_s&=\phi_{s0}\bar\rho_{s0},
 \label{eq:solid-mass}\\
 \bar J&=\frac{\bar\rho_{s0}}{\bar\rho_s},\qquad
 \phi_s=\frac{\phi_{s0}\bar J}{J},\qquad
 \phi_s a=\phi_{s0}.
 \label{eq:distention}
\end{align}
For an incompressible solid phase the intrinsic density is constant,
\(\bar\rho_s=\bar\rho_{s0}\), so \eqref{eq:distention} gives \(\bar J=1\); the
present construction is the compressible generalization of that limit.
The reference solid fraction satisfies \(0<\phi_{s0}<1\). Full saturation
gives fluid fraction \(1-\phi_s\). We retain states with positive mineral
volume and \(0<\phi_s<1\).

The phase stresses must be added in a common spatial frame. Let
\(\widehat{\mathbf\sigma}_s\) denote the intrinsic mineral Cauchy stress
in the frame reached by \(\bar{\mathbf F}\). Its representation in the
mixture frame is
\begin{equation}
 \bar{\mathbf\sigma}_s
 =\mathbf R_A\widehat{\mathbf\sigma}_s\mathbf R_A^T.
 \label{eq:rotated-mineral-cauchy}
\end{equation}
Both are stresses per current mineral volume; the rotation changes their
components, not their normalization. The intrinsic fluid stress is
\(-p\mathbf I\), with positive \(p\) denoting compression. The
volume-fraction-weighted phase balance therefore gives
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf\sigma
 =\phi_s\bar{\mathbf\sigma}_s-(1-\phi_s)p\mathbf I.
 \label{eq:constitutive-phase-stress-sum}
\end{equation}
The single-prime effective stress includes the full pore-pressure term,
\begin{align}
 \mathbf\sigma'&=\mathbf\sigma+p\mathbf I,
 \label{eq:total-cauchy-single-prime}\\
 &=\phi_s(\bar{\mathbf\sigma}_s+p\mathbf I).
 \label{eq:constitutive-single-prime-phase-stress}
\end{align}
Multiplication by reference-to-current volume ratios defines the skeleton
and mineral Kirchhoff stresses:
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf\tau'=J\mathbf\sigma',\qquad
 \bar{\mathbf\tau}_s=\bar J\bar{\mathbf\sigma}_s.
 \label{eq:kirchhoff-volume-conventions}
\end{equation}
The mineral energy \(\bar W_s(\bar{\mathbf F})\), per reference mineral
volume, supplies the true-frame Kirchhoff stress and its mixture-frame
representation through
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{\mathbf\tau}_s
 =\pder{\bar W_s}{\bar{\mathbf F}}\bar{\mathbf F}^T,\qquad
 \bar{\mathbf\tau}_s
 =\mathbf R_A\widehat{\mathbf\tau}_s\mathbf R_A^T.
 \label{eq:rotated-mineral-kirchhoff}
\end{equation}
Objectivity of the mineral energy makes these stresses symmetric, including
for an anisotropic mineral. Using \(J\phi_s=\phi_{s0}\bar J\) in the
phase balance gives
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf\tau'
 =\phi_{s0}(\bar{\mathbf\tau}_s+p\bar J\mathbf I).
 \label{eq:constitutive-kirchhoff-phase-stress}
\end{equation}
This is a spatial tensor equation. Its trace alone would determine only
mean stress; the work calculation below retains its shear components.

\section{Reversible work and the equivalent energy}
\label{sec:work-equivalence}

Write \(W_s(\mathbf F,\bar J)\) for solid energy per reference mixture
volume. At fixed temperature, pressure equilibrium and the deformation
stress follow from \citep{fosterxu2025}
\begin{equation}
 \left.\pder{W_s}{\bar J}\right|_{\mathbf F}=-\phi_{s0}p,
 \qquad
 \mathbf P'=\left.\pder{W_s}{\mathbf F}\right|_{\bar J},
 \qquad \mathbf\tau'=\mathbf P'\mathbf F^T.
 \label{eq:pressure-conjugacy}
\end{equation}
An infinitesimal variation about a finite elastic state therefore gives
\begin{equation}
 \delta W_s=\mathbf\tau':(\delta\mathbf F\mathbf F^{-1})
                 -\phi_{s0}p\,\delta\bar J.
 \label{eq:coupled-energy-work}
\end{equation}
All variations are elastic. The independent arguments in this expression
are \(\mathbf F\) and \(\bar J\).

To separate mineral work from distention work, vary
\(\mathbf F=a^{1/3}\mathbf R_A\bar{\mathbf F}\). Direct differentiation
gives
\begin{align}
 \delta\mathbf F\mathbf F^{-1}
 &=\frac13\mathbf I\,\delta\ln a
   +\delta\mathbf R_A\mathbf R_A^T
   +\mathbf R_A(\delta\bar{\mathbf F}\bar{\mathbf F}^{-1})\mathbf R_A^T,
 \label{eq:spherical-distention-virtual-deformation}\\
 \delta\bar J
 &=\bar J\tr(\delta\bar{\mathbf F}\bar{\mathbf F}^{-1}).
 \label{eq:mineral-volume-variation}
\end{align}
Differentiating \(\mathbf R_A\mathbf R_A^T=\mathbf I\) shows that
\(\delta\mathbf R_A\mathbf R_A^T\) is skew. Its contraction with the
symmetric stress \(\mathbf\tau'\) vanishes. Substitution into
\eqref{eq:coupled-energy-work} therefore gives
\begin{align}
 \delta W_s
 &=\frac13\tr\mathbf\tau'\,\delta\ln a
 +\left(\mathbf R_A^T\mathbf\tau'\mathbf R_A
                 -\phi_{s0}p\bar J\mathbf I\right):
       (\delta\bar{\mathbf F}\bar{\mathbf F}^{-1}),
 \label{eq:phase-work-substitution}\\
 &=\frac13\tr\mathbf\tau'\,\delta\ln a
 +\phi_{s0}\widehat{\mathbf\tau}_s:
       (\delta\bar{\mathbf F}\bar{\mathbf F}^{-1}).
 \label{eq:distention-mineral-energy-work}
\end{align}
The second line uses the full phase balance
\eqref{eq:constitutive-kirchhoff-phase-stress} and the stress rotation
\eqref{eq:rotated-mineral-kirchhoff}. The fluid-pressure work cancels,
leaving mineral work weighted by the reference solid fraction. This
cancellation does not require the mineral stress and strain to have common
principal directions.

The distention energy is the remaining constitutive input. We assume that it
depends only on its volume ratio, \(W_A(a)\), with no separate fabric or
orientation energy. Integrating the mineral work at fixed distention then
gives
\begin{equation}
 W_s(\mathbf F,\bar J)=W_A(a)+\phi_{s0}\bar W_s(\bar{\mathbf F}),
 \qquad a=\frac J{\bar J},\qquad
 \bar{\mathbf F}=a^{-1/3}\mathbf R_A^T\mathbf F.
 \label{eq:equivalent-mixture-mineral-energy}
\end{equation}
The distention derivative is
\(\partial W_A/\partial\ln a=\tr\mathbf\tau'/3\). Objectivity of
\(\bar W_s\) means invariance under a left rotation of its argument:
\begin{equation}
 \bar W_s(a^{-1/3}\mathbf R_A^T\mathbf F)
 =\bar W_s(a^{-1/3}\mathbf F).
 \label{eq:conformal-objective-energy}
\end{equation}
Thus \(W_s\) is a function of \(\mathbf F\) and \(\bar J\), independent
of \(\mathbf R_A\). This result holds for material anisotropy because a
left rotation leaves the material metric \(\bar{\mathbf C}\) unchanged.
The internal rotation specifies an intermediate frame, not a new elastic
mode in this constitutive specialization. We may set \(\mathbf R_A=\mathbf I\)
when evaluating the energy, provided mineral stresses computed in another
frame are transformed by \eqref{eq:rotated-mineral-kirchhoff}.

We can also differentiate this energy to check that it returns the full
phase stress. At fixed mineral volume, a change in skeleton deformation
changes distention by \(\delta\ln a=\delta\ln J\). At fixed skeleton
deformation, a change in mineral volume instead gives
\(\delta\ln a=-\delta\ln\bar J\). These two variations give
\begin{align}
 \mathbf\tau'&=\pder{W_A}{\ln a}\mathbf I
                         +\phi_{s0}\dev\bar{\mathbf\tau}_s,
 \label{eq:energy-returned-effective-stress}\\
 \pder{W_A}{\ln a}
 &=\frac{\phi_{s0}}3\tr\bar{\mathbf\tau}_s+\phi_{s0}p\bar J.
 \label{eq:energy-returned-pressure-balance}
\end{align}
Here \(\dev\bar{\mathbf\tau}_s=\bar{\mathbf\tau}_s
-\tr(\bar{\mathbf\tau}_s)\mathbf I/3\) removes the mean stress.
The second line uses pressure equilibrium, the trace form of
\eqref{eq:constitutive-kirchhoff-phase-stress}. Substituting it into the
first recovers \eqref{eq:constitutive-kirchhoff-phase-stress}, including all
shear components, so the integrated energy and the weighted phase stress
give the same response.

For a purely volumetric mineral variation,
\(\delta\bar{\mathbf F}\bar{\mathbf F}^{-1}
=\mathbf I\delta\bar J/(3\bar J)\), the same work relation reduces to
\begin{equation}
 \delta W_s=\frac13\tr\mathbf\tau'\,\delta\ln a
 +\frac{\phi_{s0}}3\tr\bar{\mathbf\sigma}_s\,\delta\bar J.
 \label{eq:equivalent-volumetric-work}
\end{equation}
This is the volume-work relation used in the scalar companion derivation.
The factor \(\phi_{s0}\) appears because the mineral energy is normalized
per reference mineral volume while the skeleton work is normalized per
reference mixture volume.

\section{Pressure coupling at finite deformation}
\label{sec:finite-biot}

At a prescribed pressure, solve \eqref{eq:anisotropic-mineral-eos} for
\(\bar J(\mathbf F,p)\). Following the pressure transformation in
\citet{fosterxu2025}, define
\begin{align}
 W''(\mathbf F,p)&=W_s(\mathbf F,\bar J(\mathbf F,p)),
 \label{eq:reduced-energy}\\
 W'(\mathbf F,p)&=W''(\mathbf F,p)+\phi_{s0}p\bar J(\mathbf F,p).
 \label{eq:legendre-energy}
\end{align}
Differentiating \(W''\) at fixed pressure gives the double-prime stress.
The derivative of \(W'\) at fixed pressure equals the derivative of
\(W_s\) at fixed mineral volume because the mineral-response terms cancel
by \eqref{eq:pressure-conjugacy}. Thus
\begin{align}
 \mathbf P''&=\left.\pder{W''}{\mathbf F}\right|_p,
 \label{eq:fixed-pressure-stress}\\
 \mathbf P'&=\left.\pder{W'}{\mathbf F}\right|_p,
 \label{eq:fixed-mineral-state-stress}\\
 &=\mathbf P''+\phi_{s0}p
       \left.\pder{\bar J}{\mathbf F}\right|_p.
 \label{eq:piola-transform-general}
\end{align}
Pushing the stresses to the current configuration gives
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf\sigma=\mathbf\sigma''-p\mathbf B,\qquad
 \mathbf B=\mathbf I-\frac{\phi_{s0}}J
       \left.\pder{\bar J}{\mathbf F}\right|_p\mathbf F^T.
 \label{eq:finite-biot-tensor}
\end{equation}
Here \(\mathbf\sigma''=J^{-1}\mathbf P''\mathbf F^T\).
At fixed pressure, the contraction of \(\mathbf B\) with the current virtual deformation
gives the pore-volume change per current mixture volume:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\delta(J-\phi_{s0}\bar J)}J
 =\mathbf B:(\delta\mathbf F\mathbf F^{-1}),\qquad \delta p=0.
 \label{eq:biot-pore-volume-variation}
\end{equation}
The numerator is the pore-volume change per reference mixture volume;
dividing by \(J\) gives the current-volume normalization.

To evaluate this derivative, differentiate the mineral equation at fixed
pressure, which gives
\begin{align}
 &\left[K_s+\left(1-\frac K{\phi_{s0}K_s}\right)p\bar J\right]
     \left.\pder{\ln\bar J}{\mathbf F}\right|_p
 \nonumber\\
 &\qquad=\frac K{\phi_{s0}}\mathbf F^{-T}
 -\frac13\left(1-\frac K{\phi_{s0}K_s}\right)
   \pder{}{\mathbf F}
   \left[\mathbf I:\mathbb{C}_s:\dev\mathbf\varepsilon\right].
 \label{eq:mineral-fixed-pressure-derivative}
\end{align}
The material logarithmic strain is
\(\mathbf\varepsilon=\tfrac12\log\mathbf C\), where
\(\mathbf C=\mathbf F^T\mathbf F\). In an anisotropic solid, the
logarithmic strain and its conjugate stress need not have the same principal
directions. The derivative of the matrix logarithm must therefore be retained.
Using its self-adjointness gives the explicit current Biot tensor
\begin{align}
 \mathbf B
 &=\mathbf I-\frac{\bar J}
 {J\left[K_s+\left(1-\dfrac K{\phi_{s0}K_s}\right)p\bar J\right]}
 \left\{K\mathbf I\right.
 \nonumber\\
 &\qquad\left.
 -\frac{\phi_{s0}}3\left(1-\frac K{\phi_{s0}K_s}\right)
 \mathbf F\left[\pder{\log\mathbf C}{\mathbf C}:
       \dev(\mathbb{C}_s:\mathbf I)\right]\mathbf F^T
 \right\}.
 \label{eq:anisotropic-biot-explicit}
\end{align}
The matrix derivative and its repeated-eigenvalue limit are given in
\cref{sec:logarithmic-derivative}. If mineral volume and shape are coupled
by \(\mathbb{C}_s\), a change of skeleton shape changes \(\bar J\).
The second term inside braces represents this contribution. It makes the
pressure response directional even though distention changes only volume and orientation.
Mineral anisotropy alone need not give directional pressure coupling.
If \(\dev(\mathbb{C}_s:\mathbf I)=\mathbf0\), as for cubic symmetry,
this term vanishes and \(\mathbf B\) remains spherical even when the
mineral shear response is anisotropic.

The energy depends on material strain, so a superposed spatial rotation
\(\mathbf Q\) leaves \(\bar J\) unchanged and rotates the tensor as
\(\mathbf B(\mathbf Q\mathbf F,p)=\mathbf Q\mathbf B(\mathbf F,p)
\mathbf Q^T\). The matrix-logarithm derivative maps symmetric tensors
to symmetric tensors; hence \(\mathbf B\) is symmetric.

The energy also provides two useful checks on the stress interpretation.
First, direct energy differentiation retains the complete phase balance:
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf\sigma
 =\frac{\phi_{s0}}J\bar{\mathbf\tau}_s
       -(1-\phi_s)p\mathbf I.
 \label{eq:total-stress-phase-energy}
\end{equation}
The mineral Kirchhoff stress is computed from its prescribed energy and
true deformation, then rotated into the mixture frame by
\eqref{eq:rotated-mineral-kirchhoff}. Equation
\eqref{eq:distention-mineral-energy-work} proves their agreement for finite
states, including states with different stress and strain directions.
Second, mixed differentiation of \(W'\) gives
\begin{align}
 \left.\pder{W'}p\right|_{\mathbf F}&=\phi_{s0}\bar J,
 \label{eq:pressure-envelope}\\
 \left.\pder{\mathbf\sigma}p\right|_{\mathbf F}&=-\mathbf B.
 \label{eq:cauchy-pressure-tangent}
\end{align}
Thus a fixed-deformation pressure experiment measures \(\mathbf B\)
through the change in total stress. On an admissible pressure interval
containing zero, integrating this derivative gives
\begin{equation}
 \mathbf\sigma(\mathbf F,p)
 =\mathbf\sigma(\mathbf F,0)
       -\int_0^p\mathbf B(\mathbf F,\pi)\,\dd\pi.
 \label{eq:integrated-pressure-response}
\end{equation}
The reduced effective stress \(\mathbf\sigma''\) itself depends
on pressure. The instantaneous coupling tensor therefore applies to an
infinitesimal pressure increment; a finite change from the drained stress
requires the integral in \eqref{eq:integrated-pressure-response}.

\section{Discussion and conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We derived the mixture energy by equating the reversible work of the
volume-fraction-weighted phase stresses to the work of mineral deformation
and distention. Writing the distention as a dilation times a proper rotation separates the
volume change from the frame rotation; the skew rotation variation performs no
work against a symmetric stress.
The mineral contribution is its prescribed energy multiplied by the
reference solid fraction. The drained spherical response then determines
the distention energy, and the complete drained Hooke law gives the
compliance restriction in \eqref{eq:drained-compliance-restriction}.

The mineral-volume equation couples mineral volume to the shape of the
skeleton through the anisotropic mineral law. Its derivative produces a
directional Biot tensor while preserving the full spatial phase stress
balance. The
isotropic reduction recovers the logarithmic scalar relation used by Gajo
and the companion manuscript. The reference stress and storage coefficients,
together with the finite unjacketed path, provide additional checks. Independent
numerical differentiation verifies the phase stress, pressure derivative,
and energy at finite anisotropic deformations. A finite-element
discretization of the coupled deformation and fluid-transport balances
reproduces the constant-coefficient consolidation reference in the
constant-tangent limit and satisfies a manufactured-solution convergence test
for that reference tangent. At finite load the nonlinear solutions approach
the reference as the load decreases, but at \(nx=20\), \(dt=10^{-3}\) their
normalized pressure discrepancy floors at about \(3.2\times10^{-3}\) rather
than decaying as the load tends to \(10^{-4}\). The floor is attributable to the fixed-step backward-Euler temporal error: the linear
step-refinement runs give \(3.7\times10^{-3}\) and \(7.1\times10^{-3}\) at
\(dt=10^{-3}\) and \(2\times10^{-3}\) (ratio \(1.94\), first order in \(dt\)),
and the fixed-mesh successive-difference temporal orders at \(nx=16/32/64\)
are \(0.98\)--\(1.40\), including values above one; these are measurements
from a sequence that retains a mesh-step cross term, and no order above one
is asserted. \Cref{fig:fe-verification} displays these measurements and
\cref{fig:fe-reference-comparison} the reference comparison. The finite-load
rotated-anisotropy, partial-drainage, and rotated-pore-fabric calculations are
reported as demonstrations, not as quantitative verification of the nonlinear
law or as experimental validation; the fabric law itself is checked at the
material point against a separate re-implementation of its equations, which
shares the section's modelling conventions.

Conformal distention requires the skeleton and mineral to share their
normalized right stretch, while allowing a relative rotation. With a
volume-only distention energy, this rotation leaves the response unchanged and
allows their compliance difference to describe only an additional spherical
strain. In particular, an isotropic mineral gives an isotropic drained
skeleton in this construction. Anisotropy caused by pore shape in an
otherwise isotropic mineral requires a law for nonspherical distention and
its work. Two independently selected anisotropic stiffnesses will generally
fail \eqref{eq:drained-compliance-restriction}; that failure identifies
missing deformation mechanisms rather than a pressure coefficient to be
chosen independently.

\Cref{sec:pore-fabric} supplies that law. Replacing the scalar dilation
with a symmetric positive definite distention \(\mathbf G\) makes the
fabric tensor a constitutive variable with its own energy
\(W_{\mathrm{dis}}(\mathbf G)\) and symmetric work conjugate
\eqref{eq:fabric-distention-stress}. The equilibrium
\eqref{eq:fabric-equilibrium} that determines the fabric generalizes the
pressure equation: its volume component recovers the conformal result
exactly, and its shape components equilibrate the fabric against the
mineral stress with no external conjugate. The rank-one drained compliance
restriction relaxes to the full fabric-symmetric distention compliance
\eqref{eq:fabric-compliance-restriction}, and the Biot tensor gains
directional coupling from the pore fabric in addition to the mineral. In
the transversely isotropic fabric case the Biot tensor has distinct axial
and transverse coefficients, predicting directionally asymmetric pressure
coupling even when the mineral itself is isotropic. This is the mechanism
by which a reaction-induced pore fabric would enter the poromechanical
response.

The conformal restriction is a constitutive specialization, not a
consequence of stress symmetry alone. In the equilibrium similarity
transformation considered by \citet[section~8.9]{drumheller2000},
symmetry of a particular true stress and its transformed stress requires
that the true stress commute with \(\mathbf A^T\mathbf A\).
Requiring the same transformation to preserve symmetry for every
symmetric stress makes \(\mathbf A^T\mathbf A\) spherical and gives
a dilation times a rotation. A constitutively restricted family of
stresses need not imply that universal condition. Symmetry removes work
against a skew rotation increment; it does not remove work against a
symmetric shape-changing increment. Volume-only distention work is the
additional mechanism prescribed here.

The examples use synthetic elastic parameters. Application to a material
requires checking the measured stiffnesses against the assumed deformation
mechanism and restricting strains to a range supported by data. Constant
coefficients in logarithmic strain define an elastic energy; measured
finite-pressure acoustic stiffnesses include the tangent about a stressed
state and need separate interpretation \citep{zha1996forsterite}.
A general distention gradient can additionally represent changes in pore
shape. Its work requires a tensorial constitutive law, which
\cref{sec:pore-fabric} supplies. Restricting an
already prescribed tensorial distention energy to a dilation times a
rotation need not recover the volume-only energy assumed here; couplings
to orientation or skeleton deformation can remain. The conformal result of
\cref{sec:finite-biot} applies locally to that specialization, and
compatibility of independently prescribed distention fields is a separate
boundary-value question. The phase-work balance is the same in both
models; only the distention law that closes it differs.
